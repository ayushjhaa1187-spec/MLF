3. Why it matters

ML is already woven into ordinary activities: weather forecasts, camera focus, inboxes, shopping, video feeds and voice assistants.

The lecture argues that ubiquity comes from solving tasks that do not yield clean hand-written rules.

Four levels for solving an input → output task

Manual labourA person directly produces the output.
Tool useA person builds or operates a tool.
ProgrammingA person writes the exact rule into software.
Machine learningA person supplies data and broad design; the system learns the rule.

Decision rule: when should you consider ML?

Can people do it?If not, manual labour is not a solution.
Can people state the rule?If yes, programming may be simpler and better.
Does a pattern exist, with enough data?If yes, ML may learn a useful approximation — even when the rule cannot be articulated.

Case studies from the lecture

Password verificationpassword → accept / rejectThe rule is explicit: compare with the saved password. Program it directly; ML is unnecessary.
Face detectionimage pixels → face locationsPeople recognise faces, but cannot precisely encode the concept from pixels. Learn from many labeled face and non-face images.
Weather predictionradar / conditions → rain or shineNature has a complex rule, but people do not know it well enough to code. Historical weather data provides the examples.

Why manual work and rules break down

Scale & speed

Humans could label spam or inspect images, but not fast enough for millions of cases.

Unclear concepts

Ideas such as a face, spam, or a spoken wake word are easy to recognize but hard to translate into an exact program.

Unknown dynamics

For weather or robot navigation, relevant rules exist but are too complex or incomplete to hand-code for every situation.

Applications: the same pattern repeated

Spam filteremail → spam / inboxLearn from examples of spam and legitimate mail.
Recommenderswatch / purchase history → likely interestsLearn taste patterns across many users and items.
Voice assistantsaudio → text → actionLearn speech and how varied phrases map to commands.
Roboticsenvironment → actionAdapt to situations that were not fully anticipated in code.
Gamesgame state → moveLearn strategies for complex decision spaces such as Go.
Marketingaudience signals → targeting choiceUse limited budget where it is most likely to have impact.
