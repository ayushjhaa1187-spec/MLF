Week 6: Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) & Positive Definiteness

The pinnacle of numerical linear algebra: factorizing arbitrary rectangular transformations into orthonormal rotations and coordinate stretchings, low-rank matrix approximations, pseudoinverses, and quadratic energy forms.

1. The SVD Theorem & Spectral Derivation

While the classical Spectral Theorem for eigendecomposition applies only to square diagonalizable matrices, the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is universally defined for any real rectangular matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$:

$$A = U \Sigma V^T$$
  • $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$: Orthonormal matrix whose columns $u_i$ are the left singular vectors (eigenvectors of $A A^T$).
  • $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$: Rectangular diagonal matrix containing ordered non-negative singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$, where $r = \text{rank}(A) \le \min(m, n)$.
  • $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$: Orthonormal matrix whose columns $v_i$ are the right singular vectors (eigenvectors of $A^T A$).
Rigorous Derivation from $A^T A$ Spectral Decomposition

Step 1: Gram Matrix Symmetry and Positive Semi-Definiteness.

Consider the Gram matrix $G = A^T A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. Notice:

$$G^T = (A^T A)^T = A^T (A^T)^T = A^T A = G \implies G \text{ is symmetric.}$$ $$\forall x \in \mathbb{R}^n, \quad x^T G x = x^T (A^T A) x = (Ax)^T (Ax) = \|Ax\|_2^2 \ge 0 \implies G \text{ is positive semi-definite.}$$

Step 2: Eigendecomposition of $A^T A$.

By the Spectral Theorem for real symmetric matrices, there exists an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with real, non-negative eigenvalues:

$$A^T A v_i = \lambda_i v_i, \quad \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_r > \lambda_{r+1} = \dots = \lambda_n = 0$$

Define the singular values as the non-negative square roots: $\sigma_i \equiv \sqrt{\lambda_i}$.

Step 3: Construction of Left Singular Vectors $u_i$.

For each non-zero singular value $\sigma_i > 0$ ($i = 1, \dots, r$), define:

$$u_i \equiv \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A v_i \in \mathbb{R}^m$$

Let us verify that the vectors $\{u_1, \dots, u_r\}$ form an orthonormal set in $\mathbb{R}^m$:

$$u_i^T u_j = \left(\frac{1}{\sigma_i} A v_i\right)^T \left(\frac{1}{\sigma_j} A v_j\right) = \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} v_i^T (A^T A v_j) = \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} v_i^T (\lambda_j v_j) = \frac{\lambda_j}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} v_i^T v_j$$

Since $\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2$ and $v_i^T v_j = \delta_{ij}$ (Kronecker delta):

$$u_i^T u_j = \frac{\sigma_j^2}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} \delta_{ij} = \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i} \delta_{ij} = \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i = j \\ 0 & \text{if } i \neq j \end{cases}$$

Step 4: Basis Completion and Factorization.

If $r < m$, extend $\{u_1, \dots, u_r\}$ to a full orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^m$ by picking $m-r$ orthonormal vectors $\{u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m\}$ from the left nullspace $\text{Null}(A^T)$. Then $A v_j = \sigma_j u_j$ holds for all $j=1, \dots, n$ (since for $j > r$, $A v_j = 0 = \sigma_j u_j$). In matrix form:

$$A V = U \Sigma \implies A = U \Sigma V^T \quad \blacksquare$$

2. Geometric Interpretation & Live Interactive Visual Lab

Geometrically, matrix multiplication $x \mapsto Ax$ is not an arbitrary distortion; it factorizes into three pure transformations:

  1. $V^T$: An orthonormal rotation/reflection in the input domain $\mathbb{R}^n$ that aligns the right singular vectors with standard cardinal axes.
  2. $\Sigma$: A pure axis-aligned stretch, scaling cardinal axes by factors $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r$ (mapping the unit sphere to an axis-aligned ellipsoid).
  3. $U$: An orthonormal rotation/reflection in the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$, reorienting the ellipsoid principal semi-axes along $u_1, \dots, u_r$.

3. Worked Numerical Walkthrough 1: Hand-Computable $2 \times 2$ Matrix

Let us compute the full SVD of symmetric matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ step by step.

Step 1: Compute Gram Matrix $A^T A$ $$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3(3) + 2(2) & 3(2) + 2(3) \\ 2(3) + 3(2) & 2(2) + 3(3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 13 & 12 \\ 12 & 13 \end{bmatrix}$$
Step 2: Characteristic Equation & Eigenvalues $$\det(A^T A - \lambda I) = (13 - \lambda)^2 - 12^2 = (13 - \lambda - 12)(13 - \lambda + 12) = (1 - \lambda)(25 - \lambda) = 0$$ $$\lambda_1 = 25, \quad \lambda_2 = 1$$

Singular values: $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{25} = 5, \quad \sigma_2 = \sqrt{1} = 1$.

Step 3: Right Singular Vectors $v_1, v_2$

For $\lambda_1 = 25$:

$$(A^T A - 25 I) v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -12 & 12 \\ 12 & -12 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_{11} \\ v_{12} \end{bmatrix} = 0 \implies v_{11} = v_{12} \implies v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

For $\lambda_2 = 1$:

$$(A^T A - 1 I) v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 12 \\ 12 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_{21} \\ v_{22} \end{bmatrix} = 0 \implies v_{21} = -v_{22} \implies v_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
Step 4: Left Singular Vectors $u_1, u_2$ $$u_1 = \frac{1}{\sigma_1} A v_1 = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$u_2 = \frac{1}{\sigma_2} A v_2 = \frac{1}{1}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
Step 5: Outer Product Dyadic Expansion & Rank-1 Approximation $$A = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^T = 5 \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 + 0.5 & 2.5 - 0.5 \\ 2.5 - 0.5 & 2.5 + 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Optimal Rank-1 approximation: $A_1 = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 & 2.5 \\ 2.5 & 2.5 \end{bmatrix}$.

Frobenius error: $\|A - A_1\|_F = \sqrt{4 \times (0.5)^2} = \sqrt{1.0} = 1.0 = \sigma_2$. Exactly matches Eckart-Young!

4. Worked Numerical Walkthrough 2: Hand-Computable $3 \times 2$ Rectangular Matrix

Consider the tall rectangular matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$.

Step 1: Compute $A^T A$ $$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1+0+1 & 0+0+1 \\ 0+0+1 & 0+1+1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$
Step 2: Eigenvalues & Singular Values $$\det(A^T A - \lambda I) = (2 - \lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1) = 0$$ $$\lambda_1 = 3 \implies \sigma_1 = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732, \quad \lambda_2 = 1 \implies \sigma_2 = 1.0$$
Step 3: Singular Vectors $V$ and $U$ $$v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad v_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} A v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$u_2 = \frac{1}{1} A v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Complete $U \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ by finding $u_3 \in \text{Null}(A^T)$ with $\|u_3\|=1$:

$$A^T u_3 = \begin{bmatrix} u_{31} + u_{33} \\ u_{32} + u_{33} \end{bmatrix} = 0 \implies u_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$
Step 4: Reconstruction Check $$U \Sigma V^T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{6}} & 0 & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = A \quad \checkmark$$

5. Low-Rank Approximation (Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem)

Representing $A$ as the dyadic sum of rank-1 matrices $A = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^T$, the Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem states that for any $k < r$, the truncated sum:

$$A_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i u_i v_i^T$$

is the strictly optimal rank-$k$ approximation under both the Spectral Norm and the Frobenius Norm:

$$\min_{\text{rank}(B) \le k} \|A - B\|_2 = \|A - A_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}$$ $$\min_{\text{rank}(B) \le k} \|A - B\|_F = \|A - A_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=k+1}^r \sigma_i^2}$$

6. Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse & Condition Numbers

When $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is not square or not full rank, its ordinary inverse $A^{-1}$ does not exist. The Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse $A^+$ provides the unique optimal minimum-norm least-squares solution:

$$A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$$

where $\Sigma^+ \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ is formed by inverting every non-zero singular value: $\Sigma^+_{ii} = 1/\sigma_i$ for $\sigma_i > 0$, and transposing.

Condition Number $\kappa(A)$ & Noise Sensitivity

The $L_2$ condition number of $A$ is $\kappa_2(A) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}} = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_r}$.

If $\sigma_r \approx 0$, then $\kappa(A) \gg 10^6$. In computing $x = A^+ b$, tiny perturbations in $b$ are magnified by $1/\sigma_r$, causing severe numerical instability. Truncated SVD (discarding small singular values below a threshold $\tau$) acts as a natural regularizer.

7. Positive Definite Matrices & Quadratic Forms

A symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is Positive Definite ($A \succ 0$) if the scalar quadratic form $q(x) = x^T A x > 0$ for all non-zero $x \neq \mathbf{0}$.

PropertyCondition for $A \succ 0$ (Positive Definite)Condition for $A \succeq 0$ (Semi-Definite)
EigenvaluesAll $\lambda_i > 0$ strictly positiveAll $\lambda_i \ge 0$ non-negative
Minors (Sylvester)All leading principal minors $\det(A_{1:k, 1:k}) > 0$All principal minors $\ge 0$
Cholesky FactorizationUnique $A = L L^T$ with $L_{ii} > 0$$A = L L^T$ with possible $L_{ii} = 0$
Contour GeometryConvex hyper-ellipsoid with strict global minimumFlat valleys / degenerate cylinders

8. Python & NumPy Implementation with Mathematical Assertions

import numpy as np

# Walkthrough 1: Symmetric 2x2 Matrix
A1 = np.array([[3.0, 2.0], [2.0, 3.0]])
U1, S1, Vt1 = np.linalg.svd(A1)
V1 = Vt1.T

# Exact singular values check
assert np.isclose(S1[0], 5.0) and np.isclose(S1[1], 1.0)
# Reconstruction assertion
assert np.allclose(A1, U1 @ np.diag(S1) @ Vt1)
# Rank-1 approximation error assertion
A1_k1 = S1[0] * np.outer(U1[:, 0], Vt1[0, :])
assert np.isclose(np.linalg.norm(A1 - A1_k1, 'fro'), S1[1])

# Walkthrough 2: Rectangular 3x2 Matrix
A2 = np.array([[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0]])
U2, S2, Vt2 = np.linalg.svd(A2, full_matrices=True)

# Singular values check
assert np.isclose(S2[0], np.sqrt(3.0)) and np.isclose(S2[1], 1.0)
# Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
A2_pinv = np.linalg.pinv(A2)
# Pseudoinverse satisfies Penrose conditions: A @ A^+ @ A == A
assert np.allclose(A2 @ A2_pinv @ A2, A2)

print("All Week 6 SVD mathematical assertions passed successfully!")
